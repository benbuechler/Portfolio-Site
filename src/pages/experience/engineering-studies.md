# Taking the Long Way to Engineering

## The idea

For as long as I can remember, I've been fascinated by how things work. I've always loved building, taking things apart, and finding ways to make things better. Engineering seemed like a natural fit, but for a long time, I didn't believe I was capable of pursuing it.

During my final years of high school, I struggled a lot with self-doubt. I questioned my academic abilities and convinced myself that engineering was something other people were capable of, but not me. Rather than challenge that belief, I chose what felt like a safer path and pursued a degree in business.

I went on to study Management and Organizational Studies at Western University, specializing in Finance and Administration. I learned a tremendous amount, met some incredible people, and gained valuable experience working in the corporate world. But as I progressed through my degree and into my career, I couldn't shake the feeling that something was missing.

## Making the change

After spending time working in the corporate world, I began to realize that the lifestyle and career I had been working toward weren't necessarily what I wanted.

I enjoyed solving problems, collaborating with people, and working on challenging projects, but I missed the hands-on creativity that had always excited me. I wanted to understand how things worked, not just how businesses operated. More importantly, I wanted to create things.

The idea of going back to school for engineering had always been in the back of my mind. But making that decision meant leaving behind a career I had already invested years into building, returning to being a full-time student, and confronting the same doubts that had held me back in high school.

Eventually, I realized that I would rather try and struggle than spend the rest of my life wondering what could have been.

So I took the leap.

## Challenging myself

I enrolled in Electrical Engineering Technology at Fanshawe College, and I can confidently say it has been one of the best decisions of my life.

It hasn't been easy. In fact, I'm challenging myself academically more than I ever have before. Between calculus, electronics, programming, and the countless hours spent troubleshooting circuits that refuse to work, there are plenty of moments when I find myself completely outside my comfort zone.

**But that's exactly where I want to be.**

For the first time in a long time, I genuinely look forward to what I'm learning. The late nights, difficult assignments, and frustrating problems are all worth it because I'm working toward something I'm truly passionate about.

Beyond the classroom, I've found opportunities I never would have imagined. Becoming President of the Fanshawe Engineering Club and Chair of the IEEE Student Branch has allowed me to connect with other students who share that same curiosity and excitement for engineering. I've had the opportunity to help build a community, organize industry experiences, and take on projects that push me even further.

I'm no longer just trying to get through school. I'm trying to get everything I possibly can out of it.

## Looking back

One of the biggest lessons this experience has taught me is that **the path you start on doesn't have to be the path you finish on.**

For years, I let the fear of not being good enough influence what I believed I could accomplish. Looking back, I wish I had given myself more credit. But I also wouldn't change the path that brought me here. My experience in business gave me skills, perspectives, and opportunities that I know will make me a better engineer.

I still experience self-doubt. I still encounter problems that make me question whether I'm capable of figuring them out. The difference now is that I don't let those feelings make my decisions for me.

I'm learning that being challenged doesn't mean I'm in the wrong place. Sometimes, it means I'm exactly where I need to be.

## A message to anyone reading this

If there's one thing I hope someone takes away from my experience, it's this:

**Don't ever convince yourself that you're not smart enough to pursue something you're passionate about.**

For years, I believed that intelligence was some fixed quality that determined what I could and couldn't accomplish. I looked at people pursuing engineering and assumed they had something I didn't.

What I've come to realize is that success has far less to do with being naturally gifted than I once thought. It's about how much time, effort, and persistence you're willing to put into learning something. Some things might take you longer to understand than others, and that's okay. Struggling with something doesn't mean you're incapable of it. It means you're learning.

And here's the part that changed everything for me: **when you genuinely love what you're doing, putting in that time and effort stops feeling like a sacrifice.**

I can spend hours working through a difficult problem, troubleshooting a circuit, or learning something completely new, and lose track of time. Not because it's easy, but because I genuinely enjoy the process.

I wish I could go back and tell my younger self that he was capable of so much more than he believed.

I can't do that, but maybe someone reading this is having those same doubts right now.

**Don't let the fear of not being smart enough stop you from finding out what you're capable of. Give yourself the chance to try. You might surprise yourself.**

Going back to school meant starting over in a lot of ways, but I've never been more excited about where I'm headed. I'm challenging myself more than ever before, and I'm loving every minute of it.
